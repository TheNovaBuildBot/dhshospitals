    <p style="font-size:1.1rem;line-height:1.8">Kidney stones are common in Gujarat. Hot summers, heavy sweating and not drinking enough water make urine concentrated, which lets minerals crystallise into stones. Many people first learn they have a stone when sudden, severe pain sends them to the emergency room. This guide explains the symptoms, which signs need urgent care, how stones are treated, and how to lower your chances of getting another one.</p>

    <h2>What Does Kidney Stone Pain Feel Like?</h2>
    <p>A stone can sit quietly in the kidney for a long time. Pain usually starts when it moves into the <strong>ureter</strong>, the narrow tube between the kidney and the bladder. Typical symptoms include:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li>Sudden, severe pain in the back or side, below the ribs, often coming in waves</li>
      <li>Pain spreading to the lower abdomen or groin</li>
      <li>Pink, red or brown urine (blood in the urine)</li>
      <li>Burning while passing urine, or needing to pass urine often</li>
      <li>Nausea and vomiting</li>
    </ul>

    <div style="background:var(--primary-light);border-left:4px solid var(--primary);padding:20px 24px;border-radius:0 var(--radius) var(--radius) 0;margin:24px 0">
      <p style="font-weight:700;font-size:1.05rem;margin-bottom:12px;color:var(--primary)">Go to the emergency department straight away if you have:</p>
      <ul style="margin:0 0 0 20px;line-height:2">
        <li><strong>Fever or chills with stone pain</strong>, which can mean an infection behind a blocked kidney</li>
        <li><strong>Pain so severe</strong> you cannot sit still or find relief</li>
        <li><strong>Vomiting</strong> that stops you keeping fluids down</li>
        <li><strong>Very little or no urine</strong></li>
        <li>A stone and <strong>only one working kidney</strong></li>
      </ul>

    <h2>How Are Kidney Stones Diagnosed?</h2>
    <p>Your doctor will examine you and usually advise a <strong>urine test</strong> and <strong>blood tests</strong> to check for infection and kidney function. Imaging such as an <strong>ultrasound</strong> or <strong>CT scan</strong> shows the size and position of the stone, which decides the treatment.</p>

    <h2>Treatment Options</h2>
    <h3>Small stones: medicines and fluids</h3>
    <p>Many small stones pass on their own over days to weeks. Your doctor may prescribe pain relief, medicine that relaxes the ureter to help the stone pass, and plenty of fluids. Follow-up scans confirm that the stone has passed.</p>

    <h3>Larger or stuck stones: minimally invasive procedures</h3>
    <p>Stones that are too large to pass, are blocking the kidney, keep causing pain or come with infection need a procedure. Today most stones are treated with <strong>minimally invasive techniques</strong>, without a large cut. Depending on the stone's size and position, a urologist may use shock waves from outside the body, a thin telescope passed through the urinary tract to break and remove the stone, or a small keyhole opening in the back for large kidney stones. Your urologist will explain which approach suits you and why.</p>

    <h2>Preventing Kidney Stones</h2>
    <p>About half of people who have had a stone will have another within some years. These habits help:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li><strong>Drink enough water</strong> to pass pale yellow urine, more in summer and after sweating</li>
      <li><strong>Cut down on salt</strong>, including pickles, papad and packaged snacks</li>
      <li><strong>Do not stop dairy</strong> on your own; normal dietary calcium usually helps prevent stones</li>
      <li><strong>Limit excess animal protein</strong> and sugary drinks</li>
      <li><strong>Ask about stone analysis</strong>, as knowing the stone type guides prevention</li>
    </ul>
