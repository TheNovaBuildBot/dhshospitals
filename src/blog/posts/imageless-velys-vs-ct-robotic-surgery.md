    Written by <strong><a href="/doctors/doctor-hardik/" style="color: #1a6fc4; text-decoration: none;">Dr. Hardik M Shah</a></strong>, MBBS (Gold Medal), MS Orthopedic, FRCS (Germany) &nbsp;|&nbsp;

  <p>Robotic knee replacement surgery has moved from novelty to mainstream in India — but not all robots work the same way. When families in Ahmedabad research their options, they often see brand names like Mako, ROSA, and VELYS listed side by side, as if they are interchangeable. They are not.</p>

  <p>The single most important technical distinction between these systems is whether they require a CT scan before surgery — or whether they map your actual anatomy <em>during</em> the procedure. That difference has real consequences for your radiation exposure, your wallet, your surgical timeline, and how well your implant fits your knee on the day you actually go under anaesthesia.</p>

  <p>At <strong>DHS Multispecialty Hospital, Gurukul, Ahmedabad</strong>, lead orthopaedic surgeon <strong>Dr. Hardik M. Shah</strong> chose the <strong>VELYS Robotic-Assisted Solution by Johnson &amp; Johnson DePuy Synthes</strong> specifically because it is <strong>imageless</strong>. This post explains exactly what that means, why it matters, and how it compares to every alternative available in Gujarat today.</p>

  <!-- SECTION 1 -->
  <h2 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.55rem; margin-top: 44px; border-left: 4px solid #1a6fc4; padding-left: 14px;">What Does "Imageless" Actually Mean?</h2>

  <p>In everyday language, "imageless" simply means the robot does not rely on pre-taken images — specifically a CT scan — to plan or guide your surgery. To a family who has never seen a surgical robot, that might sound like cutting corners. It is precisely the opposite.</p>

  <p>Traditional CT-based robotic systems like <strong>Mako (Stryker)</strong>, <strong>ROSA (Zimmer Biomet)</strong>, and <strong>Cuvis</strong> require the patient to undergo a full CT scan weeks before the operation. That scan is fed into the robot's planning software, which generates a virtual model of your bone anatomy. The surgeon studies this model, plans bone cuts digitally, and on surgery day, the robot executes those pre-planned cuts.</p>

  <p><strong>VELYS does something fundamentally different.</strong> Instead of working from a scan taken days ago, it registers your actual bone anatomy <em>inside the operating theatre</em>, in real time, using specialised tracking probes and an infrared optical navigation system. It builds a live 3D model of your specific knee the moment surgery begins — no scan, no radiation, no pre-planning window.</p>

  <div style="background: #e8f4fd; border-radius: 10px; padding: 24px 28px; margin: 28px 0;">
    <h3 style="color: #003087; font-size: 1.1rem; margin: 0 0 14px;">Why Real-Time Registration Matters More Than Pre-Planned</h3>
    <ul style="margin: 0; padding-left: 20px; line-height: 2.1;">
      <li>Your body is not the same from one day to the next. Swelling, fluid, and soft tissue tension in the knee can shift between your CT scan date and your surgery date.</li>
      <li>A pre-planned model is a snapshot of your knee from the past. VELYS works with your knee as it exists <em>right now</em>, under anaesthesia, with actual soft tissue tension in the room.</li>
      <li>Real-time registration allows the surgical team to detect and respond to intraoperative surprises — bone quality variations, cartilage loss patterns — that no pre-operative scan could fully predict.</li>
      <li>The surgeon can update the plan mid-procedure. A robot locked into a CT-based blueprint cannot course-correct without significant workflow interruption.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <p><strong>Imageless does not mean less information.</strong> It means more <em>current</em>, more <em>relevant</em> information, gathered at the exact moment decisions are made.</p>

  <!-- SECTION 2 -->
  <h2 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.55rem; margin-top: 44px; border-left: 4px solid #1a6fc4; padding-left: 14px;">The CT-Scan Burden: What Older Robots Ask of You</h2>

  <p>Before a patient can have surgery with a Mako, ROSA, or Cuvis robot, they must complete a mandatory pre-operative CT scan. This step is not optional — it is built into the clinical workflow of these systems. For patients in Ahmedabad who are already managing pain, limited mobility, and the logistical demands of surgical preparation, this adds a significant burden.</p>

  <div style="background: #fff5f5; border-left: 4px solid #c0392b; border-radius: 8px; padding: 24px 28px; margin: 28px 0;">
    <h3 style="color: #8b0000; font-size: 1.1rem; margin: 0 0 14px;">What CT-Based Robotic Surgery Requires Before You Enter the OR</h3>
    <ul style="margin: 0; padding-left: 20px; line-height: 2.1;">
      <li><strong>Separate CT scan appointment:</strong> You must travel to a radiology centre — adding an additional trip and wait time, inconvenient especially for elderly patients with limited mobility.</li>
      <li><strong>Radiation exposure:</strong> A musculoskeletal CT scan of the knee and lower limb carries an effective dose of approximately <strong>0.3–1.0 mSv</strong> — comparable to several months of background radiation, and cumulative if you have had prior imaging.</li>
      <li><strong>Additional cost:</strong> CT scans for robotic surgical planning in India typically cost between <strong>₹8,000 and ₹15,000</strong>, charged separately from the surgery cost itself.</li>
      <li><strong>Waiting period of 1–2 weeks:</strong> Once the scan is completed, the data must be processed and loaded into the robot's planning software before your surgical date can be confirmed.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <p>Beyond logistics and cost, there is a <strong>clinical limitation</strong> that CT-based planning cannot resolve: the problem of temporal mismatch. Your CT scan creates a model of your knee as it was on the day of the scan. But on the morning of surgery — which may be 10 to 14 days later — your knee's soft tissue tension, ligament laxity, and cartilage state will not be identical. The robot, however, is still executing a plan based on that older snapshot.</p>

  <p><strong>A static plan cannot adapt to a dynamic body.</strong> This is not a flaw in any surgeon's skill — it is a fundamental limitation of any system that locks its plan before the patient is on the table.</p>

  <p>Understanding the VELYS intraoperative process demystifies what "real-time registration" looks like in practice. This is a structured, surgeon-guided sequence that happens inside the operating theatre, before the first bone cut is made.</p>

The surgeon reviews this model before proceeding.</p>

Before any bone is cut, the surgeon uses the robot to test ligament balance dynamically across the full range of knee flexion. The system provides real-time data on gap symmetry and tension, allowing implant size and positioning to be fine-tuned to achieve optimal soft tissue equilibrium — with your actual ligaments, under actual surgical conditions.</p>

The result: implant fit tuned to your soft tissue tension as it exists at that exact moment in the OR.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.55rem; margin-top: 44px; border-left: 4px solid #1a6fc4; padding-left: 14px;">Is Imageless Surgery Right for Me? Your Questions Answered</h2>

  <details style="border: 1px solid #d0e8f8; border-radius: 8px; margin-bottom: 12px; background: #f7fbff;">
    <summary style="padding: 16px 20px; font-weight: 700; color: #0a3d62; cursor: pointer; font-size: 1rem;">Does "imageless" mean less accurate?</summary>
    <div style="padding: 4px 20px 18px; color: #333; line-height: 1.75;">
      <p>No — and this is the most common misconception. Imageless means no CT scan is required, not less precise. VELYS achieves sub-millimetre accuracy by registering your actual bone anatomy intraoperatively. The result is a surgical plan based on your knee as it truly exists on the day of surgery — not a scan taken weeks earlier.</p>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details style="border: 1px solid #d0e8f8; border-radius: 8px; margin-bottom: 12px; background: #f7fbff;">

The large majority of such cases are entirely suitable for VELYS. Your suitability is confirmed at your consultation with clinical examination and standard X-rays. No CT scan is needed for this assessment either.</p>
    </div>

  <details style="border: 1px solid #d0e8f8; border-radius: 8px; margin-bottom: 12px; background: #f7fbff;">
    <summary style="padding: 16px 20px; font-weight: 700; color: #0a3d62; cursor: pointer; font-size: 1rem;">Is VELYS suitable for partial knee replacement?</summary>
