    <p style="font-size:1.1rem;line-height:1.8">"The doctor has asked for an ECG and an echo. Are they the same thing? Do I need a TMT too?" Heart tests have short, confusing names, and it is natural to wonder why you need more than one. Each test answers a different question about your heart. This guide explains what each one shows, how it is done, and the symptoms that mean you should see a cardiologist soon.</p>

    <h2>ECG (Electrocardiogram): The Heart's Electrical Signal</h2>
    <p>An <strong>ECG</strong> records the electrical activity that makes your heart beat. Small sticky pads are placed on your chest, arms and legs, and a machine draws your heart rhythm as a line on paper or a screen. It takes a few minutes and is completely painless.</p>
    <p>An ECG can show an irregular or very fast or slow heartbeat, signs of a current or earlier heart attack, and strain on the heart muscle. It is usually the <strong>first test</strong> for chest pain, palpitations or dizziness, because it is quick and gives an immediate picture.</p>

    <h2>2D Echo (Echocardiography): A Moving Picture of the Heart</h2>
    <p>A <strong>2D Echo</strong> is an ultrasound scan of the heart, similar to the scan used in pregnancy. A probe with gel is moved across your chest, and a live image of your heart appears on the screen. There is no radiation and no pain.</p>
    <p>Where an ECG shows the electrical signal, an echo shows the <strong>structure and pumping</strong>: how strongly the heart squeezes, whether the valves open and close properly, and whether any chamber is enlarged. It is commonly used for breathlessness, swollen ankles, a heart murmur, or to check the heart after a heart attack.</p>

    <h2>TMT (Treadmill Stress Test): How the Heart Copes with Effort</h2>
    <p>Some heart problems only show up when the heart is working hard. In a <strong>TMT</strong>, you walk on a treadmill while your ECG and blood pressure are recorded. The speed and slope increase every few minutes, and the test stops when you reach the target heart rate or if you have symptoms.</p>
    <p>A TMT helps find narrowed heart arteries that reduce blood flow during exertion, often felt as chest discomfort or breathlessness while climbing stairs or walking fast. Your cardiologist decides whether a TMT is safe and useful for you; it is not done during active chest pain.</p>

    <h2>Holter Monitoring: Catching Symptoms That Come and Go</h2>
    <p>Palpitations or dizzy spells often disappear by the time you reach the clinic. A <strong>Holter monitor</strong> is a small portable ECG recorder you wear for 24 to 48 hours while you go about your normal day. It captures heart rhythm problems that a few minutes of ECG can miss.</p>

    <h2>When Should You See a Cardiologist?</h2>
    <p>Book a cardiology consultation if you notice:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li>Chest pain, pressure or tightness, especially on exertion</li>
      <li>Breathlessness while walking or climbing stairs</li>
      <li>Palpitations, dizziness or fainting</li>
      <li>Swelling of the feet and ankles</li>
      <li>A family history of heart disease, or diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol</li>
    </ul>
