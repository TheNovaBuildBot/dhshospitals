  <!-- INTRO -->
  <p>The most common fear we hear from patients considering knee replacement is not about the surgery itself — it is about <strong>the recovery</strong>. How much will it hurt? How long will I be dependent on others? When will I get my life back?</p>

  <p>With <strong>VELYS imageless robotic knee replacement at DHS Multispecialty Hospital</strong>, the answers are more reassuring than most patients expect. This guide gives you an honest, week-by-week picture — not a best-case scenario, but a realistic look at what the majority of our patients experience.</p>

  <h2 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.55rem; margin-top: 44px; border-left: 4px solid #1a6fc4; padding-left: 14px;">Why VELYS Enables a Faster Recovery Timeline</h2>

  <p>Recovery timeline is not just about willpower or physiotherapy compliance — it starts in the operating theatre. <strong>How precisely the implant is placed directly determines how quickly the body accepts it and relearns to move.</strong></p>

  <p>Three features of VELYS contribute to DHS's 48-hour discharge standard and early mobilisation protocol:</p>

  <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
    <li><strong>Sub-millimetre bone cuts:</strong> Less bone trauma means less bleeding, less post-operative inflammation, and a knee that starts healing with minimal disruption to surrounding tissue.</li>
    <li><strong>Real-time ligament balancing:</strong> When the implant is placed with optimal soft tissue tension from the first day, the knee feels stable and natural — patients are less hesitant to bear weight early.</li>
    <li><strong>Minimal soft tissue disturbance:</strong> Precision robotic cuts mean the surgical team disturbs as little tissue as possible, reducing swelling and the period of reactive pain that follows.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Recovery is not linear — there will be good days and stiff mornings. But the overall trajectory, with VELYS at DHS, is consistently faster than conventional approaches.</p>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 0;">Anaesthesia and Surgery</h3>
    <p>Most patients receive <strong>spinal (regional) anaesthesia</strong> — you remain awake but feel nothing below the waist. Some patients prefer general anaesthesia; this is discussed and decided with the anaesthesiologist before surgery. Spinal anaesthesia is preferred because it results in less post-operative nausea and allows earlier mobilisation.</p>

    <p>The surgery itself takes <strong>60 to 90 minutes</strong>. You will be in a sterile operating theatre with Dr. Shah, the VELYS robotic system, an anaesthesiologist, and a scrub nurse team. VELYS first maps your knee anatomy, then guides the robotic arm through precision bone cuts, and finally Dr. Shah seats the J&amp;J Attune implant components.</p>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">Recovery Room (1–2 Hours Post-Op)</h3>
    <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
      <li>You are transferred to the recovery room where nurses monitor blood pressure, oxygen levels, and pain every 15 minutes</li>
      <li>The spinal anaesthesia will wear off over 2–4 hours — you will start to feel sensation returning from the feet upward</li>
      <li>Pain management begins immediately: nerve blocks (if used), IV analgesics, and anti-inflammatory medications</li>
      <li>Ice packs and limb elevation reduce swelling from the first hour</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">Same Evening: Early Mobilisation Preparation</h3>
    <p>On the <strong>same evening as surgery</strong>, the DHS physiotherapist visits bedside for initial exercises — ankle pumps (moving the foot up and down to keep blood circulating), deep breathing exercises, and static quadriceps contractions. These are not painful, but they are important: they reduce blood clot risk and signal to the nervous system that the joint is ready to begin working.</p>

    <p>Dr. Shah personally briefs the patient's family <strong>within 30 minutes</strong> of the procedure completing — about how the surgery went, the implant seated, and what to expect in the next 24 hours.</p>

    <div style="background: #e8f4fd; border-radius: 8px; padding: 14px 18px; margin-top: 18px;">
      <strong>What to expect:</strong> Drowsiness for 3–6 hours post-op, manageable pain (well-controlled by medication), some nausea if general anaesthesia was used. Your family can be with you in the room by evening.
    </div>
  </div>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 0;">Walking Begins</h3>
    <p>This is the milestone that surprises most patients and families: <strong>the majority of VELYS patients at DHS take their first assisted steps within 18–24 hours of surgery.</strong> This is not exceptional — it is the standard.</p>

    <p>The DHS physiotherapist assists you to stand at the bedside, using a wheeled walker for support. The first session lasts 5–10 minutes. You walk 3–5 steps, stand, and return to bed. That is enough for Day 1.</p>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">What Walking on Day 1 Feels Like</h3>
    <p>"The knee will feel stiff and swollen — this is completely normal," explains Dr. Shah. "Patients sometimes expect it to feel like the new knee is already working smoothly. Day 1, it feels more like there is a tight bandage around a joint that hasn't woken up yet. The important thing is that bearing weight doesn't cause sharp pain — it causes pressure and stiffness, which is very different."</p>

    <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
      <li><strong>Pain level on Day 1:</strong> 4–6 out of 10 is typical, well-controlled by the scheduled medication protocol. If pain is higher, alert the nursing team immediately — this is a signal to adjust the analgesic regime, not to stop mobilisation.</li>
      <li><strong>Swelling:</strong> Expected. Your knee will look larger than normal. Elevation of the leg and ice (20 minutes on, 20 off) every 2–3 hours helps manage this.</li>
      <li><strong>First meal:</strong> Light solid food is usually permitted 6–8 hours after surgery — earlier for spinal anaesthesia cases. Staying well-hydrated is especially important.</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">Day 1 Physiotherapy Session</h3>
    <p>In addition to the walking trial, your Day 1 physiotherapy includes: standing balance exercises at the bedside, static knee straightening (pressing the back of the knee down against the mattress), and gentle heel slides. These are all done from the bed and standing position — no strenuous bending yet.</p>

    <div style="background: #e8f4fd; border-radius: 8px; padding: 14px 18px; margin-top: 18px;">
      <strong>DHS milestone:</strong> By end of Day 1, most patients have walked with a walker, completed a physiotherapy session, and eaten a full meal. Family members are encouraged to be present and learn the early exercise routine.
    </div>
  </div>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 0;">DHS 48-Hour Discharge Standard</h3>
    <p>Most DHS patients are discharged <strong>within 48 hours</strong> of surgery — Day 2 or Day 3 morning. This is a clinical outcome, not a cost-cutting measure. Evidence consistently shows that patients who go home sooner, in a familiar environment, with a family support network, progress through early recovery better than those kept in hospital longer.</p>

    <p>Before discharge, you must meet the following clinical criteria:</p>

    <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
      <li>Walking independently with a walker for a minimum of <strong>15–20 metres</strong></li>
      <li>Able to manage one flight of stairs with support (tested with physiotherapist before discharge)</li>
      <li>Pain controlled comfortably on oral medications — no IV analgesia required</li>
      <li>No active signs of infection: wound dry, no excessive redness, no fever above 37.8°C</li>
      <li>No signs of deep vein thrombosis (DVT): no calf swelling or pain</li>
      <li>Able to use the toilet independently (with grab rail support)</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">Setting Up Your Home Before Surgery</h3>
    <p>We recommend preparing your home before admission, so the transition is seamless. These simple changes make the first week dramatically more comfortable:</p>

    <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
      <li><strong>Raised toilet seat</strong> (4–6 inches) — makes sitting and standing significantly easier</li>
      <li><strong>Non-slip bath mat</strong> in bathroom and at bedside</li>
      <li><strong>Remove low furniture and trip hazards:</strong> rugs, mats, loose cables</li>
      <li><strong>Temporary bed on ground floor</strong> if your bedroom is on an upper floor</li>
      <li><strong>A firm armchair</strong> (not a deep sofa) for sitting — easier to rise from</li>
      <li><strong>Caregiver support:</strong> a family member who can assist with meals and medications for the first 1–2 weeks</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 20px;">When to Call Your Surgeon — Red Flag Signs</h3>
    <div style="background: #fff5f5; border-left: 4px solid #c0392b; border-radius: 6px; padding: 16px 20px; margin-top: 14px;">
      <strong style="color: #8b0000;">Call DHS immediately (+91 9081610444) if you notice:</strong>
      <ul style="margin-top: 10px; line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
        <li>Fever above 38.5°C at any point in first 6 weeks</li>
        <li>Redness, warmth, or any discharge from the wound</li>
        <li>Calf pain, swelling, or redness — possible DVT sign</li>
        <li>Severe pain not controlled by prescribed medications</li>
        <li>Knee significantly more swollen than the day before (after Day 4)</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 24px;">Managing Swelling and Stiffness</h3>
    <ul style="line-height: 2.1; color: #333;">
      <li><strong>Ice therapy:</strong> Apply an ice pack (wrapped in a thin cloth) for 20 minutes, 3–4 times daily, for the first 2 weeks. Especially after physiotherapy sessions.</li>
      <li><strong>Elevation:</strong> When resting, keep the leg elevated on a pillow — under the calf, not under the knee (this is important — placing the pillow under the knee creates a flexion contracture)</li>
      <li><strong>Avoid prolonged sitting:</strong> Stand and take a few steps every 45–60 minutes when awake</li>
      <li><strong>Compression bandage:</strong> As instructed by your physiotherapist — helps manage circumferential swelling</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 24px;">Medication Management</h3>
    <p>At discharge, you will receive a clear medication schedule — typically oral analgesics (paracetamol combination), anti-inflammatory (COX-2 inhibitor), and a blood thinner injection or tablet for DVT prevention. From Day 7, most patients begin to reduce analgesic use to as-needed only rather than on a fixed schedule — a sign the recovery is progressing normally.</p>

    <h3 style="color: #1a6fc4; font-size: 1.1rem; margin-top: 24px;">Range of Motion Milestone (Day 14)</h3>
