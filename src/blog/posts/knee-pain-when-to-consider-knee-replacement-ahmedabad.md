    <p style="font-size:1.1rem;line-height:1.8"><strong>Knee pain is one of the most common reasons older adults come to our orthopedic clinic in Ahmedabad.</strong> Most of it is manageable without surgery. Some of it is a quiet sign of something that needs a closer look. And a smaller group of patients eventually reaches the point where knee replacement is the right answer — not because anyone pushed them toward it, but because everything short of surgery has stopped working.</p>

    <p>This guide is for that whole spectrum. We will walk through why knees ache as we age, the warning signs you should not ignore, the conservative treatments we try first, and how we know — together — when it is time to consider knee replacement at DHS Multispecialty Hospital.</p>

    <h2>Why knees ache as we age</h2>
    <p>The knee is the largest weight-bearing joint in the body, and it is asked to do a lot — flex, extend, rotate slightly, and absorb roughly three to four times your body weight with every step. Inside the joint, the ends of the thigh bone (femur), shin bone (tibia) and kneecap (patella) are covered by a smooth, slippery layer of <strong>articular cartilage</strong>. Between the femur and tibia sit two C-shaped shock absorbers called the <strong>menisci</strong>. Ligaments stabilise the joint, muscles power it, and a thin lining (the synovium) keeps it lubricated.</p>

    <p>From around the fifth decade of life onward, the cartilage slowly wears thin. Genetics, body weight, old injuries, the kind of work the knees have done, and underlying conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or diabetes all change how fast that happens. As cartilage thins, the bones underneath start to bear load they were never designed to bear directly. That is the basis of <strong>osteoarthritis</strong>, the single most common reason for knee pain in older adults.</p>

    <h2>When is knee pain just ageing — and when is it something more?</h2>
    <p>Many older adults have some background knee discomfort. The honest answer is that not every twinge needs imaging or a specialist. But there are clear markers that move pain out of the "just ageing" bucket into something that deserves a careful look:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li>Pain that wakes you at night, even when the knee is still.</li>
      <li>Pain that is making you avoid stairs, slow you down on flat ground, or change the way you walk.</li>
      <li>Persistent swelling around the kneecap or in the back of the knee.</li>
      <li>A clicking or grinding sensation that is new or getting louder.</li>
      <li>A noticeable bend in the leg (the knee turning inward or outward) that was not there before.</li>
      <li>Pain that no longer settles with rest and over-the-counter pain medication.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Red flags that mean you should see a specialist now</h2>
    <p>Some symptoms should not wait. Please see an orthopedic specialist — at DHS or elsewhere — promptly if you experience any of the following:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li><strong>The knee locks or gives way</strong> when you walk, and you have lost confidence on stairs.</li>
      <li><strong>Sudden severe pain or swelling</strong> after a fall, a twist, or no obvious injury at all.</li>
      <li><strong>The knee is red, warm and very tender</strong>, especially with fever — this can be an infection.</li>
      <li><strong>A clear deformity</strong> in the leg after a fall — a possible fracture.</li>
      <li><strong>Pain combined with calf swelling or shortness of breath</strong> — these can be signs of a clot.</li>
      <li><strong>You have lost so much function</strong> you can no longer climb a flight of stairs, walk to the toilet at night, or perform basic daily activities.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Conservative options doctors try first</h2>
    <p>For most older adults with knee pain, the right first step is <em>not</em> surgery. A senior orthopedic team will work through several layers of treatment before the conversation ever turns to replacement:</p>

    <h3>1. Lifestyle adjustments and weight</h3>
    <p>Every additional kilogram of body weight translates into roughly three to four kilograms of extra load through the knee. Even modest weight loss often reduces pain noticeably. Low-impact activities — swimming, cycling on a stationary bike, walking on level ground in good shoes — protect the joint while keeping the muscles around it strong.</p>

    <h3>2. Physiotherapy</h3>
    <p>A structured course of physiotherapy is one of the highest-value treatments for knee pain in older adults. The right exercises strengthen the quadriceps and hamstrings, improve balance, restore range of motion and reduce the load through the worn part of the joint. We routinely combine clinic-based sessions with a home programme so patients keep the gains going.</p>

    <h3>3. Medication, when appropriate</h3>
    <p>Paracetamol is usually the first-line option. NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) can help short-term but must be used carefully in older adults — they can affect the stomach, kidneys, blood pressure and heart. Any patient on long-term medication or with diabetes, hypertension or kidney issues should review their pain medicines with an orthopedist and a physician.</p>

    <h3>4. Intra-articular injections</h3>
    <p>For some patients we offer joint injections — corticosteroid (for a flare), hyaluronic acid (for moderate osteoarthritis), or PRP (platelet-rich plasma) in selected cases. These can buy meaningful relief, sometimes for months, but they do not regrow cartilage. They are a bridge, not a destination.</p>

    <h3>5. Bracing, aids and orthotics</h3>
    <p>For patients with single-compartment osteoarthritis, an offloader brace can shift load away from the worn side of the joint. Walking sticks (on the opposite side of the painful knee), good shoes and small lifestyle changes — a slightly raised toilet seat, a stair railing — can preserve independence for years.</p>

    <h2>When is knee replacement the right answer?</h2>
    <p>Knee replacement is a planned surgery, not an emergency. We start the conversation when several things are true at once:</p>
    <ul style="margin:10px 0 18px 24px;line-height:1.9">
      <li>X-rays (and sometimes MRI) show <strong>advanced osteoarthritis</strong> — bone-on-bone or near-bone-on-bone changes, often with loss of joint space, bone spurs and a visible deformity.</li>
      <li>Pain is interfering with <strong>everyday life</strong> — sleep, daily walks, social activities, work, or care for a partner or grandchildren.</li>
      <li><strong>Conservative care has been honestly tried</strong> — weight, physiotherapy, medication, injections, bracing — and the relief is no longer enough.</li>
      <li>The patient is medically fit for surgery, or can be made fit with help from cardiology, anaesthesia, diabetology or nephrology if needed.</li>
      <li>The patient wants the surgery — clearly understands the benefits, the risks, the recovery timeline, and the lifestyle changes that come with a replaced knee.</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Knee replacement at the right time, in the right patient, is one of the most effective surgeries modern medicine offers. Patients sleep through the night again, walk to the temple or the market without thinking about pain, and often regret only that they waited too long.</p>

    <h2>What changes with robotic-assisted knee replacement</h2>
    <p>For the patients who do reach the point of replacement, a senior surgeon may offer <a href="/velys-robotic-knee-replacement-ahmedabad/">VELYS robotic-assisted knee replacement</a> — an imageless robotic platform from Johnson &amp; Johnson DePuy Synthes that DHS uses at our Gurukul hospital. The robot does not perform the surgery; the surgeon does. What the robot adds is a real-time, computer-verified plan, intra-operative ligament balance assessment, and an instrument that guides bone cuts to sub-millimetre precision.</p>

    <p>Robotic-assisted technique is not the right answer for every patient, and the DHS team performs both robotic and conventional total, partial and revision knee replacement. The choice is made jointly, after a face-to-face consultation, with a clear-eyed look at the X-ray, the patient's anatomy, and the patient's priorities.</p>

    <h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

    <details class="faq-item" open>
      <summary>Is knee pain normal at 60 or 70?</summary>
      <div class="faq-answer">Some background knee discomfort is common in older adults because of natural wear in the cartilage, and not every twinge needs imaging or surgery. But pain that interferes with sleep, daily walking, stairs or basic activities is not something to live with — it deserves an orthopedic opinion. Early review often means more options (physiotherapy, weight, injections) before surgery becomes the only answer.</div>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>How do I know if I need knee replacement?</summary>
      <div class="faq-answer">You need knee replacement when several things line up: X-rays show advanced osteoarthritis (bone-on-bone), the pain interferes with everyday life, you have honestly tried conservative care (weight, physiotherapy, medication, injections), and you are medically fit for surgery. The decision is always made jointly between you and an orthopedic specialist — it is never a one-visit recommendation.</div>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>What is the best non-surgical treatment for knee arthritis?</summary>
      <div class="faq-answer">There is no single best treatment — most patients benefit from a combination: modest weight loss, a structured physiotherapy programme, sensible pain medication, occasional joint injections, and small lifestyle adjustments. A senior orthopedist will tailor the mix to your X-ray, your weight, your medical conditions, and how active you want to be.</div>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>Is robotic knee replacement better than traditional?</summary>
      <div class="faq-answer">Robotic-assisted knee replacement is not automatically "better"; it is a tool that adds a computer-verified surgical plan and sub-millimetre cut precision. In experienced hands, both robotic and conventional knee replacement deliver excellent outcomes. The right choice depends on your knee anatomy, the kind of implant best suited to you, and surgeon preference. At DHS we perform both, and we recommend the approach that fits the patient.</div>
    </details>

    <details class="faq-item">
      <summary>How long does knee replacement recovery take?</summary>
      <div class="faq-answer">Most patients stand and walk aided on the day of surgery, are discharged in 2–3 days, walk without aids in 1–2 weeks, return to normal daily activities (including driving) in 4–6 weeks, and reach full recovery with complete range of motion in 3–4 months. Structured physiotherapy at DHS is part of every knee replacement programme.</div>
    </details>
